Glucose is soluble — why is that a problem?

Glucose dissolves in water, the same way sugar dissolves when stirred into a glass.

Sugar dissolves invisibly into water

What would happen if a plant cell just kept storing lots of dissolved glucose?

Correct. A build-up of dissolved glucose lowers the water potential inside the cell, so water moves in by osmosis — the three cells below show that gradient in stages.
1. High glucose — low water potential
2. Water moves in by osmosis
3. Cell swells — could burst
This is exactly why plants convert glucose into insoluble starch for storage instead — starch doesn't dissolve, so it has no effect on osmosis.

Does photosynthesis need chlorophyll?

What is the white part of the leaf lacking?
Variegated leaf — green + white patches

What is the white part of this leaf lacking?

Chlorophyll. Without it, that part of the leaf has no chloroplasts to absorb light — it can't photosynthesise even though the rest of the leaf can.

How could we prove CO₂ is needed?

Control — sealed with water
What colour change would each plant show with iodine?
Experiment (soda lime)
Control (water)
Soda lime plant: orange-brown. Control: blue-black. The soda lime absorbed all the CO₂, so that plant couldn't photosynthesise despite plenty of light.
Core Practical 5 — the importance of CO₂
  1. Destarch two plants by placing them in a dark cupboard for 24 hours.
  2. Place one in a sealed bell jar with water — the control.
  3. Place the other in a sealed bell jar with soda lime or sodium hydroxide.
  4. Place both plants in front of a light source for six hours.
  5. Take a leaf from each plant and test for starch.
  6. Boil for two minutes — stops any chemical reactions.
  7. Boil in ethanol (water bath) for five minutes — removes chlorophyll.
  8. Wash with water to remove the ethanol.
  9. Place on a white tile and add iodine.
Model results
Soda lime plant — no starch
Control — starch present
The soda-lime absorbs all the CO₂ in the bell jar, so even with sunlight, no photosynthesis occurs. The control is kept in exactly the same conditions except the independent variable (soda lime vs water) — that's what proves CO₂, and not something else, caused the difference.

The effect of light intensity on rate of photosynthesis

The apparatus
Bubbles from pondweed (Elodea), lamp on a ruler track
Method
  1. Set up the apparatus as shown.
  2. Add 30 cm³ of sodium hydrogen carbonate to the water (provides CO₂).
  3. Cut a 7 cm piece of Elodea pondweed.
  4. Place the pondweed in the boiling tube, cut end facing upwards.
  5. Decide on five distances for the light source.
  6. At the first distance, leave the pondweed 5 minutes to acclimatise.
  7. Count the number of bubbles in one minute.
  8. Move the lamp to the next distance, acclimatise again for 5 minutes.
  9. Count the bubbles again.
  10. Repeat, moving the lamp 10 cm further each time.

As the lamp moves further away, what happens to the rate of photosynthesis?

Correct — and here's the deeper pattern: light intensity follows an inverse square law. Double the distance and the intensity drops to a quarter (1/2² = 0.25), not a half. To analyse results properly, square the distance and plot bubbles against that — it's easier if you convert cm to m first.

Drawing graphs — the rules
  • Independent variable (what you changed) → X-axis.
  • Dependent variable (what you measured) → Y-axis.
  • Choose an appropriate scale.
  • Label both axes with units.
  • Double-check every point is plotted correctly.
  • Give the graph a title.
Exam-style question

Why do we use a thermometer in this investigation? (3 marks)

Full-mark answer: (1) records/measures the temperature, (2) checks it isn't changing, (3) because rate of reaction changes with temperature — temperature must be the control variable, or a change in bubbling could be wrongly blamed on light.
